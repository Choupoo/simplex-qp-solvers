\section{Introduction}
Let $I_1,\ldots,I_m$ be pairwise disjoint subsets whose union is $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.  The feasible set is the Cartesian product of simplices
\begin{equation}
\Kset = \left\{x\in\R^n : \sum_{i\in I_k}x_i=1 \; (k=1,\ldots,m),\; x_i\ge 0 \; (i=1,\ldots,n)\right\}.
\end{equation}
The primal problem is
\begin{equation}
\tag{P}
\min_{x\in\Kset} f(x)=x^TQx+q^Tx,
\qquad Q\succeq 0.
\end{equation}
The set $\Kset$ is nonempty, compact and convex.  Since $Q\succeq0$, $f$ is convex and continuously differentiable, with
\begin{equation}
\nabla f(x)=2Qx+q.
\end{equation}
Therefore, an optimal solution exists. Since the problem is convex, the first-order optimality conditions are also sufficient for global optimality~\cite{boyd2004convex}.

\subsection{Blockwise KKT condition}
For a given feasible $x$, define $g=\nabla f(x)$.  On block $I_k$, the normal cone of the simplex gives the condition
\begin{equation}
 g_i \ge \tau_k \quad (i\in I_k),
 \qquad x_i(g_i-\tau_k)=0 \quad (i\in I_k),
\end{equation}
for some scalar $\tau_k$.  Equivalently, positive variables in the same block must have equal minimum gradient, while zero variables may have larger gradient.  The implementation reports the residual
\begin{equation}
\max_k \max_{i\in I_k} |x_i(g_i-\min_{j\in I_k}g_j)|
\end{equation}
combined with feasibility residuals for block sums and nonnegativity.  This is the residual used to verify that A1 and A2 solve the same problem up to numerical tolerance.

\section{A1: Lagrangian dual decomposition}
\subsection{Equivalent factorized model}
Because $Q\succeq0$, write $Q=R^TR$.  In the experiments the instances are generated directly through $R$.  When \(Q\) is provided directly, the implementation uses a symmetric
eigenvalue decomposition $Q=V\operatorname{diag}(\sigma)V^T$ and sets
\begin{equation}
R=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\sigma_i})V_+^T,
\label{eq:psd-factorization}
\end{equation}
where only eigenvalues larger than a numerical tolerance are retained.  The objective becomes
\begin{equation}
 x^TQx+q^Tx=\|Rx\|_2^2+q^Tx.
\end{equation}
Introduce a linking variable $y\in\R^r$ and rewrite (P) as
\begin{equation}
\tag{P$_R$}
\min_{x\in\Kset,\;y\in\R^r}\; \|y\|_2^2+q^Tx
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y=Rx.
\end{equation}
This formulation is equivalent to (P).  The constraint $y=Rx$ is the only coupling between the quadratic term and the simplex variables, and is therefore a natural target for Lagrangian relaxation.

\subsection{Dualizing the linking equation}
Use a free multiplier $\lambda\in\R^r$ for $Rx-y=0$.  The Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
L(x,y,\lambda)=\|y\|_2^2+q^Tx+\lambda^T(Rx-y).
\end{equation}
For fixed $\lambda$, minimization over $y$ is explicit:
\begin{equation}
\min_y \; \|y\|_2^2-\lambda^Ty
= -\frac14\|\lambda\|_2^2,
\qquad y(\lambda)=\frac12\lambda.
\label{eq:y-subproblem}
\end{equation}
The remaining $x$-subproblem is linear over a product of simplices:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x\in\Kset} (q+R^T\lambda)^Tx.
\label{eq:x-subproblem}
\end{equation}
It separates by blocks.  If
\begin{equation}
 j_k(\lambda)\in\argmin_{i\in I_k}\{q_i+(R^T\lambda)_i\},
\label{eq:block-oracle}
\end{equation}
then an exact minimizer is the vertex $x(\lambda)$ defined by $x_{j_k}=1$ for every block and all other entries equal to zero.  Thus the dual function is
\begin{equation}
\psi(\lambda)
= -\frac14\|\lambda\|_2^2
+\sum_{k=1}^m \min_{i\in I_k}\{q_i+(R^T\lambda)_i\}.
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
This is a finite concave nonsmooth function on $\R^r$.  A supergradient is
\begin{equation}
 s(\lambda)=Rx(\lambda)-\frac12\lambda.
\label{eq:supergradient}
\end{equation}
Indeed, for any $\mu$, the definition of $x(\lambda)$ and $y(\lambda)$ as minimizers of $L(\cdot,\cdot,\lambda)$ gives
\begin{align}
\psi(\mu)&\le L(x(\lambda),y(\lambda),\mu) \\
&= L(x(\lambda),y(\lambda),\lambda) + (\mu-\lambda)^T(Rx(\lambda)-y(\lambda)) \\
&= \psi(\lambda)+(\mu-\lambda)^Ts(\lambda),
\end{align}
which is exactly the supergradient inequality for a concave function.  The Lagrangian dual problem is
\begin{equation}
\tag{D}
\max_{\lambda\in\R^r}\psi(\lambda).
\end{equation}
Since (PR) is convex, the linking constraints are affine, and the product of simplices has a nonempty relative interior, the relative-interior constraint qualification holds. Strong duality therefore applies~\cite{boyd2004convex}, and consequently \[ \max(D)=\min(P). \]
\subsection{A target-value deflected subgradient method}
To cast the dual problem in the minimization framework of Theorem 4.6 of d'Antonio and Frangioni~\cite[p.~382]{dantoniofrangioni}, we rewrite the dual maximization problem as
\begin{equation}
\min_{\lambda\in\R^r}\Phi(\lambda),
\qquad
\Phi(\lambda):=-\psi(\lambda).
\label{eq:phi-problem}
\end{equation}
Using the expression of the dual function in~\eqref{eq:dual},
\begin{equation}
\Phi(\lambda)
=\frac14\|\lambda\|^2
-\sum_{k=1}^m\min_{i\in I_k}
\left\{q_i+(R^T\lambda)_i\right\}.
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
The function $\Phi$ is finite, strongly convex and coercive.  For a fixed multiplier $\lambda$, let $x(\lambda)$ be any exact solution of the blockwise Lagrangian subproblem. Then
\begin{equation}
g(\lambda)
=-s(\lambda)
=\frac12\lambda-Rx(\lambda)
\in\partial\Phi(\lambda)
\label{eq:phi-subgradient}
\end{equation}
is an exact subgradient of $\Phi$.

One of the assumptions of Theorem~4.6 is that the search directions are uniformly bounded. Rather than assuming that the multiplier sequence remains in a bounded region, the algorithm explicitly projects every iterate onto a ball guaranteed to contain the unconstrained dual optimum.

Define
\begin{equation}
\rho=2\|R\|_F\sqrt m,
\qquad
B_\rho=\{\lambda\in\R^r:\|\lambda\|\le\rho\}.
\label{eq:dual-ball}
\end{equation}
Restricting the dual problem to $B_\rho$ does not change its optimal solution or optimal value. Indeed, since $\Phi$ is strongly convex and coercive, it has a unique unconstrained minimizer $\lambda^*$.

To characterize the optimality condition, observe that
\[
\Phi(\lambda)
=\frac14\|\lambda\|^2
-\min_{x\in\Kset}(q+R^T\lambda)^Tx.
\]
By the subdifferential formula for a pointwise maximum of affine functions,
\begin{equation}
\partial\Phi(\lambda)
=\frac12\lambda
-R\,\operatorname{conv}\left(
\argmin_{x\in\Kset}(q+R^T\lambda)^Tx
\right).
\label{eq:phi-subdifferential}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the optimality condition $0\in\partial\Phi(\lambda^*)$ implies that there exists
\[
\widehat x\in
\operatorname{conv}\left(
\argmin_{x\in\Kset}(q+R^T\lambda^*)^Tx
\right)
\subseteq\Kset
\]
such that
\[
0=\frac12\lambda^*-R\widehat x.
\]
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
\lambda^*=2R\widehat x.
\label{eq:lambda-opt-representation}
\end{equation}
For every $x\in\Kset$, each block belongs to a simplex and hence
\[
\|x_{I_k}\|_2\le\|x_{I_k}\|_1=1.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\|x\|^2
=\sum_{k=1}^m\|x_{I_k}\|^2
\le\sum_{k=1}^m\|x_{I_k}\|_1^2
=m.
\]
Using~\eqref{eq:lambda-opt-representation},
\begin{equation}
\|\lambda^*\|
\le2\|R\|_2\|\widehat x\|
\le2\|R\|_2\sqrt m
\le2\|R\|_F\sqrt m
=\rho.
\label{eq:lambda-opt-bound}
\end{equation}
Thus $\lambda^*\in B_\rho$, so the restriction to $B_\rho$ leaves the optimum unchanged. The orthogonal projection onto the ball is explicit:
\begin{equation}
\proj_{B_\rho}(z)=
\begin{cases}
z,&\|z\|\le\rho,\\[1mm]
\dfrac{\rho}{\|z\|}z,&\|z\|>\rho.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ball-projection}
\end{equation}

The method uses the diminishing and square-summable stepsize
\begin{equation}
\nu_k=\frac{\nu_0}{k^p},
\qquad \nu_0>0,
\qquad \frac12<p\le1.
\label{eq:diminishing-step}
\end{equation}
This choice satisfies
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu_k=\infty,
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu_k^2<\infty,
\label{eq:step-condition}
\end{equation}
which is condition~(4.1) in d'Antonio and Frangioni~\cite[p.~375]{dantoniofrangioni}.

Let
\begin{equation}
\Phi_k=\Phi(\lambda_k),
\qquad
\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^k=\min_{1\le j\le k}\Phi_j
\label{eq:record-phi}
\end{equation}
denote the current value and the best value obtained up to iteration $k$. Define
\begin{equation}
\delta_k=\frac{\delta_0}{k^\eta},
\qquad \delta_0>0,
\qquad \eta>0,
\qquad
\Phi_{\mathrm{lev}}^k=\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^k-\delta_k.
\label{eq:target-level}
\end{equation}
Since $\delta_k>0$ and $\delta_k\to0$, threshold condition~(4.16) of d'Antonio and Frangioni~\cite[p.~381]{dantoniofrangioni} is satisfied.

At the first iteration, set $\alpha_1=1$ and $d_1=g_1$. For $k\ge2$, define
\begin{equation}
\zeta_k=
\frac{\nu_{k-1}\|d_{k-1}\|^2}
{\Phi_k-\Phi_{\mathrm{lev}}^k+\nu_{k-1}\|d_{k-1}\|^2}.
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
Because
\[
\Phi_k-\Phi_{\mathrm{lev}}^k
=\Phi_k-\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^k+\delta_k>0,
\]
the denominator is positive and $0\le\zeta_k\le1$. The implementation selects
\begin{equation}
\alpha_k=\max\{\alpha_{\min},\zeta_k\},
\qquad 0<\alpha_{\min}\le1,
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
and constructs the deflected direction
\begin{equation}
d_k=\alpha_kg_k+(1-\alpha_k)d_{k-1}.
\label{eq:deflected-direction}
\end{equation}
Since both $\alpha_{\min}$ and $\zeta_k$ belong to $[0,1]$,
\[
0\le\zeta_k\le\alpha_k\le1.
\]
Thus~\eqref{eq:alpha}--\eqref{eq:deflected-direction} satisfy the target-value deflection rule~(4.15) of d'Antonio and Frangioni~\cite[p.~381]{dantoniofrangioni}.

To make the correspondence with the cited framework explicit, in the notation of d'Antonio and Frangioni~\cite[eqs.~(1.2), (1.5), and (1.6), pp.~358--360]{dantoniofrangioni}, identify
\[
X=B_\rho,
\qquad
f=\Phi,
\qquad
x_k=\lambda_k,
\qquad
\bar g_k=g_k,
\qquad
v_k=d_{k-1}.
\]
Because the previous iteration selects $d_{k-1}=\widetilde d_{k-1}$, this choice of $v_k$ is one of the alternatives admitted in~(1.5). The new deflected direction is
\[
\widetilde d_k
=\alpha_k\bar g_k+(1-\alpha_k)v_k,
\]
and the implementation selects the admissible unprojected alternative
\[
d_k=\widetilde d_k.
\]
Feasibility with respect to $B_\rho$ is enforced by the orthogonal projection in the iterate update. Therefore, the proposed update is covered by Theorem 4.6.
Finally, the multiplier update is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{k+1}
=\proj_{B_\rho}\left(\lambda_k-\nu_kd_k\right).
\label{eq:lambda-update}
\end{equation}
The primal upper bound is not used in either the stepsize or the deflection rule. It is used only to maintain a feasible primal incumbent and to compute the primal--dual stopping certificate.

\subsection{Verification of the convergence theorem}
The assumptions of Theorem~4.6 are verified as follows.

\paragraph{Convex model and feasible set.}
The function $\Phi$ is finite and convex.  The set $B_\rho$ is nonempty, closed and convex, and it contains the unconstrained minimizer by~\eqref{eq:lambda-opt-bound}.

\paragraph{Exact oracle.}
The $y$-subproblem is solved by~\eqref{eq:y-subproblem}, and every $x$-block is solved exactly by~\eqref{eq:x-subproblem}--\eqref{eq:block-oracle}.  Thus the oracle error in the notation of~\cite{dantoniofrangioni} is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_k=0\qquad\text{for every }k.
\label{eq:zero-oracle-error}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Bounded subgradients and directions.}
Because the update projects onto $B_\rho$,
\begin{align}
\|g_k\|
&=\left\|\frac12\lambda_k-Rx(\lambda_k)\right\|\\
&\le\frac12\|\lambda_k\|+\|R\|_2\|x(\lambda_k)\|\\
&\le\frac\rho2+\|R\|_2\sqrt m\\
&\le\rho.
\end{align}
Furthermore, $d_1=g_1$, and~\eqref{eq:deflected-direction} is a convex combination because $0<\alpha_k\le1$.  By induction,
\begin{equation}
\|d_k\|\le\rho\qquad\text{for every }k.
\label{eq:bounded-directions}
\end{equation}
This proves condition~(4.10) instead of assuming it.  The condition is relevant because the standard projected subgradient recursion contains $\nu_k^2\|d_k\|^2$; bounded directions together with $\sum_k\nu_k^2<\infty$ make the accumulated squared-step contribution finite.

\paragraph{Stepsize, deflection and threshold conditions.}
Equations~\eqref{eq:diminishing-step}--\eqref{eq:step-condition} satisfy condition~(4.1); equations~\eqref{eq:zeta}--\eqref{eq:deflected-direction} satisfy rule~(4.15); and~\eqref{eq:target-level} satisfies condition~(4.16).

Consider first the infinite sequence obtained when the certified-gap stopping
test and the iteration cap are disabled. For that sequence, all assumptions of
Theorem~4.6 are satisfied. The theorem gives
\[
\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\infty}\le\Phi^*+\sigma^*.
\]
Here $\sigma^*=0$ by~\eqref{eq:zero-oracle-error}, while every record value is at least $\Phi^*$.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^k\longrightarrow\Phi^*.
\label{eq:record-convergence}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, define
\begin{equation}
LB_k=\max_{1\le j\le k}\psi(\lambda_j)
=-\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}^k.
\label{eq:dual-lb}
\end{equation}
Using strong duality,
\begin{equation}
LB_k\longrightarrow\psi^*=p^*.
\label{eq:lb-convergence}
\end{equation}
The individual values $\psi(\lambda_k)$ need not increase monotonically.  The record sequence $LB_k$ is monotone by definition and is the sequence covered by the convergence statement.

The cited theorem establishes asymptotic convergence but does not provide an explicit convergence rate for this variant.

\subsection{Primal feasible upper bounds and stopping rule}

Every Lagrangian vertex $x(\lambda_k)$ belongs to $\Kset$, and
therefore
\begin{equation}
p^*\le f(x(\lambda_k)).
\label{eq:vertex-ub}
\end{equation}
The implementation also forms the running average
\begin{equation}
\bar x_k
=
\frac1k\sum_{j=1}^{k}x(\lambda_j).
\label{eq:average-x}
\end{equation}
Since $\Kset$ is convex, $\bar x_k\in\Kset$, so
$f(\bar x_k)$ is another valid primal upper bound.

Let
\[
\mathcal C_k
=
\{\bar x_0\}
\cup
\left\{
x(\lambda_j),\bar x_j:
1\le j\le k
\right\}
\]
be the set of feasible primal candidates available after iteration
$k$. The best feasible incumbent and the corresponding upper bound are
defined by
\begin{equation}
x_{\mathrm{best},k}
\in
\argmin_{x\in\mathcal C_k}f(x),
\qquad
UB_k
=
f(x_{\mathrm{best},k})
=
\min\left\{
f(\bar x_0),
\min_{1\le j\le k}
\left\{
f(x(\lambda_j)),f(\bar x_j)
\right\}
\right\}.
\label{eq:dual-phase-ub}
\end{equation}
Together with the record dual lower bound
\[
LB_k
=
\max_{1\le j\le k}\psi(\lambda_j),
\]
weak duality yields
\begin{equation}
LB_k\le p^*\le UB_k.
\label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $UB_k-LB_k$ is a valid primal--dual optimality
certificate. The upper bound is used only to maintain a feasible
primal incumbent and to evaluate the stopping criterion; it is not
used in the stepsize, the deflection rule, or the convergence proof
of the dual method.

After the dual loop, a conditional-gradient, or Frank--Wolfe, polishing phase is run from the best feasible incumbent $x_{\mathrm{best},k}$~\cite{frank1956algorithm}. For a feasible
point $x$, let
\[
h=\nabla f(x)=2R^T(Rx)+q.
\]
The linear minimization oracle is
\begin{equation}
s
\in
\argmin_{z\in\Kset}h^Tz,
\label{eq:fw-oracle}
\end{equation}
which again separates by blocks and requires one minimum search in
each block. Define the conditional-gradient direction
\[
d=s-x.
\]
Because both $x$ and $s$ belong to $\Kset$, every update
\[
x^+=x+\gamma d,
\qquad
\gamma\in[0,1],
\]
remains feasible.

The Frank--Wolfe gap at $x$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{FW}}(x)
=
h^T(x-s)
=
-h^Td.
\label{eq:fw-gap}
\end{equation}
Since $s$ minimizes $h^Tz$ over $\Kset$ and $x\in\Kset$, this
quantity is nonnegative. Moreover, for every $z\in\Kset$, convexity
gives
\[
f(x)-f(z)
\le
\nabla f(x)^T(x-z)
\le
\Delta_{\mathrm{FW}}(x),
\]
so the Frank--Wolfe gap is also a valid first-order optimality
certificate for the polishing problem.

Using $Q=R^TR$, let
\[
a=d^TQd=\|Rd\|^2.
\]
If $a>0$, exact line search along the feasible segment gives
\begin{equation}
\gamma^*
=
\proj_{[0,1]}
\left(
-\frac{h^Td}{2a}
\right)
=
\proj_{[0,1]}
\left(
-\frac{\nabla f(x)^Td}{2d^TQd}
\right).
\label{eq:fw-line-search}
\end{equation}
If $a=0$, the objective is affine along the segment. In that case,
the algorithm selects $\gamma=1$ when $h^Td<0$ and $\gamma=0$
otherwise. Therefore, every polishing iteration preserves feasibility
and cannot increase the primal objective value.

The polishing phase terminates when
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{FW}}(x)
\le
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{FW}},
\label{eq:fw-stop}
\end{equation}
or when the polishing iteration limit $T_{\mathrm{pol}}$ is reached.

The dual loop terminates successfully when
\begin{equation}
\frac{UB_k-LB_k}
{\max\{1,|UB_k|\}}
\le
\varepsilon.
\label{eq:gap-stop}
\end{equation}
If the dual iteration limit $T_{\max}$ is reached first, the current
valid lower and upper bounds are retained without claiming that the
requested tolerance has been attained. A small subgradient norm alone
is not used as a stopping criterion, because it does not provide the
required primal--dual certificate.

After the polishing phase, the best feasible incumbent and its upper
bound are updated whenever the polished point has a smaller objective
value. The final relative gap is then recomputed as
\[
\mathrm{gap}
=
\frac{UB-LB}{\max\{1,|UB|\}}.
\]
The history $\mathcal H$ records the iterations of the dual phase;
the final returned values additionally include any improvement
obtained during conditional-gradient polishing.

\subsection{A1 pseudocode}

\begin{breakablealgorithm}
\caption{A1: target-value deflected dual subgradient method with
conditional-gradient polishing}
\label{alg:a1}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}

\Require
$R,q,\mathcal I=\{I_j\}_{j=1}^{m},
T_{\max},\nu_0,p,\delta_0,\eta,\alpha_{\min},\varepsilon,
T_{\mathrm{pol}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{FW}}$

\Ensure
Feasible primal point $x_{\mathrm{best}}$, upper bound $UB$,
lower bound $LB$, relative gap, and dual history $\mathcal H$

\Statex
\Statex \textbf{Phase I: target-value deflected dual method}

\State $\rho\gets2\|R\|_F\sqrt{m}$
\State $\lambda_1\gets0$
\State $LB\gets-\infty$
\State $\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\gets+\infty$
\State $\mathcal H\gets[\,]$

\State Initialize $\bar x_0$ by setting
$(\bar x_0)_i\gets1/|I_j|$ for every $i\in I_j$
\State $x_{\mathrm{best}}\gets\bar x_0$
\State $UB\gets f(x_{\mathrm{best}})$

\For{$k\gets1$ \textbf{to} $T_{\max}$}

    \State $c_k\gets q+R^T\lambda_k$
    \State $x_k\gets0$

    \For{$j\gets1$ \textbf{to} $m$}
        \State
        $i_j\in\argmin_{i\in I_j}(c_k)_i$
        \Comment{exact block oracle}
        \State $(x_k)_{i_j}\gets1$
    \EndFor

    \State
    $\displaystyle
    \psi_k
    \gets
    -\frac14\|\lambda_k\|^2
    +\sum_{j=1}^{m}(c_k)_{i_j}$

    \State $\Phi_k\gets-\psi_k$
    \State $g_k\gets\frac12\lambda_k-Rx_k$

    \State $LB\gets\max\{LB,\psi_k\}$
    \State
    $\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}
    \gets
    \min\{\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}},\Phi_k\}$

    \State
    $\displaystyle
    \bar x_k
    \gets
    \frac{k-1}{k}\bar x_{k-1}
    +\frac1k x_k$

    \State $F_k\gets f(x_k)$
    \State $\bar F_k\gets f(\bar x_k)$

    \If{$F_k<UB$}
        \State $UB\gets F_k$
        \State $x_{\mathrm{best}}\gets x_k$
    \EndIf

    \If{$\bar F_k<UB$}
        \State $UB\gets\bar F_k$
        \State $x_{\mathrm{best}}\gets\bar x_k$
    \EndIf

    \State
    $\displaystyle
    \mathrm{gap}_k
    \gets
    \frac{UB-LB}{\max\{1,|UB|\}}$

    \State
    Append $(k,\psi_k,LB,UB,\mathrm{gap}_k)$
    to $\mathcal H$

    \If{$\mathrm{gap}_k\le\varepsilon$}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf

    \If{$k=1$}
        \State $\alpha_k\gets1$
        \State $d_k\gets g_k$
    \Else
        \State
        $\displaystyle
        \delta_k\gets\frac{\delta_0}{k^\eta}$

        \State
        $\Phi_{\mathrm{lev}}^k
        \gets
        \Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}-\delta_k$

        \State
        $\displaystyle
        \zeta_k
        \gets
        \frac{
            \nu_{k-1}\|d_{k-1}\|^2
        }{
            \Phi_k-\Phi_{\mathrm{lev}}^k
            +\nu_{k-1}\|d_{k-1}\|^2
        }$

        \State
        $\alpha_k
        \gets
        \max\{\alpha_{\min},\zeta_k\}$

        \State
        $d_k
        \gets
        \alpha_k g_k+(1-\alpha_k)d_{k-1}$
    \EndIf

    \State
    $\displaystyle
    \nu_k\gets\frac{\nu_0}{k^p}$

    \State
    $z_k\gets\lambda_k-\nu_kd_k$

    \State
    $\lambda_{k+1}
    \gets
    \proj_{B_\rho}(z_k)$

\EndFor

\Statex
\Statex \textbf{Phase II: conditional-gradient polishing}

\State $x\gets x_{\mathrm{best}}$

\For{$t\gets0$ \textbf{to} $T_{\mathrm{pol}}-1$}

    \State
    $h\gets2R^T(Rx)+q$

    \State $s\gets0$

    \For{$j\gets1$ \textbf{to} $m$}
        \State
        $\ell_j\in\argmin_{i\in I_j}h_i$
        \Comment{linear minimization oracle}
        \State $s_{\ell_j}\gets1$
    \EndFor

    \State $d^{\mathrm{FW}}\gets s-x$

    \State
    $\Delta_{\mathrm{FW}}
    \gets
    h^T(x-s)$

    \If{$\Delta_{\mathrm{FW}}\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{FW}}$}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf

    \State
    $a\gets\|Rd^{\mathrm{FW}}\|^2$

    \If{$a>0$}
        \State
        $\displaystyle
        \gamma
        \gets
        \proj_{[0,1]}
        \left(
        -\frac{h^Td^{\mathrm{FW}}}{2a}
        \right)$
    \Else
        \If{$h^Td^{\mathrm{FW}}<0$}
            \State $\gamma\gets1$
        \Else
            \State $\gamma\gets0$
        \EndIf
    \EndIf

    \State
    $x\gets x+\gamma d^{\mathrm{FW}}$

    \State
    $F\gets f(x)$

    \If{$F<UB$}
        \State $UB\gets F$
        \State $x_{\mathrm{best}}\gets x$
    \EndIf

\EndFor

\State
$\displaystyle
\mathrm{gap}
\gets
\frac{UB-LB}{\max\{1,|UB|\}}$

\State
\Return
$x_{\mathrm{best}},UB,LB,\mathrm{gap},\mathcal H$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{Cost per iteration}

In one iteration of the dual phase, the products
$R^T\lambda_k$ and $Rx_k$ dominate the computational work. With
sparse storage, each product costs $O(\nnz(R))$; for a dense matrix
$R\in\R^{r\times n}$, each product costs $O(rn)$. The blockwise
minimum search examines every component once and therefore costs
$O(n)$. The target-value scalar calculations cost $O(1)$, while the
projection onto $B_\rho$ costs $O(r)$. Consequently, one iteration
of the dual phase costs
\begin{equation}
O(\nnz(R)+n+r)
\label{eq:sparse-cost}
\end{equation}
in the sparse case and
\[
O(rn+n+r)
\]
in the dense case.

In one iteration of the conditional-gradient polishing phase, the
gradient
\[
\nabla f(x)=2R^T(Rx)+q
\]
requires the products $Rx$ and $R^T(Rx)$. Computing the line-search
coefficient additionally requires $Rd$. Therefore, one polishing
iteration has the same leading-order sparse complexity,
\[
O(\nnz(R)+n),
\]
and dense complexity
\[
O(rn+n).
\]
The memory requirement is dominated by the storage of $R$ and the
current primal and dual vectors.

The convergence theorem for the dual method is asymptotic and does
not provide a worst-case iteration bound for this variant. The total
running time therefore depends on the number of dual iterations
performed before the certified-gap test or $T_{\max}$ is reached,
together with at most $T_{\mathrm{pol}}$ conditional-gradient
iterations.

\section{A2: general-purpose solver}

\subsection{Direct constrained formulation}

Let $A\in\R^{m\times n}$ be the block-incidence matrix,
\[
A_{ki}=\begin{cases}1,&i\in I_k,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
Then A2 solves the original convex QP directly:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x\in\R^n}\;x^TQx+q^Tx,
\qquad
Ax=\mathbf 1,
\qquad
x\ge0.
\label{eq:a2-formulation}
\end{equation}
The code supplies the exact objective and gradient \[ f(x)=\|Rx\|^2+q^Tx, \qquad \nabla f(x)=2R^T(Rx)+q, \] together with the linear equalities and nonnegativity bounds to SciPy's SLSQP implementation. SLSQP is a sequential quadratic programming method originally implemented by Kraft \cite{kraft1988slsqp}, while the software implementation used here is provided by SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}.

\subsection{Initialization and solution validation}

Since problem~(P) is convex, every feasible point satisfying the KKT
conditions is globally optimal. The output of A2 is therefore
validated through the objective value, the blockwise KKT residual
defined in Section~1.1, the maximum block-sum violation, and the
maximum nonnegativity violation.

Two A2 configurations are used in the numerical experiments.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A2-cold:} SLSQP is initialized independently of A1
    at the uniform feasible point
    \[
    (x^{0}_{\mathrm{cold}})_i
    =
    \frac{1}{|I_k|},
    \qquad i\in I_k,
    \quad k=1,\ldots,m.
    \]

    \item \textbf{A2-warm:} SLSQP is initialized at the polished
    feasible point returned by A1:
    \[
    x^{0}_{\mathrm{warm}}
    =
    x_{\mathrm{A1}}.
    \]
\end{itemize}

Both configurations use exactly the same objective function, exact
gradient, equality constraints, bound constraints, and SLSQP options.
In particular, both use
\[
\texttt{ftol}=10^{-10}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
T_{\mathrm{A2}}=800.
\]
Hence, the initial point is the only algorithmic difference between
the two A2 runs.

A2-cold measures the independent performance of the general-purpose
solver. A2-warm measures the benefit of refining a solution already
produced by A1. The wall-clock time of A2-warm alone contains only the
refinement phase and excludes the cost of producing the initial point.
For this reason, the report also considers the end-to-end pipeline
time
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
=
t_{\mathrm{A1}} + t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}.
\label{eq:pipeline-time}
\end{equation}

The A2-warm time must therefore not be interpreted as the independent
running time of A2 or compared directly with the total running time of
A1 without also reporting~\eqref{eq:pipeline-time}.

\subsection{A2 pseudocode}

\begin{breakablealgorithm}
\caption{A2: cold-start and A1 warm-start constrained solves}
\label{alg:a2}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}

\Require
$R,q,\{I^j\}_{j=1}^{m}$,
polished A1 solution $x^{A1}$,
maximum iterations $T^{A2}_{\max}$,
tolerance $\varepsilon_{A2}$

\Ensure
A2-cold result, A2-warm result, pipeline time, solver statuses,
and residuals

\State Build $A\in\R^{m\times n}$ with
$A_{ji}=1$ for $i\in I^j$

\State Construct the uniform feasible point $x^{\mathrm{unif}}$

\For{$j=1,\ldots,m$}
    \For{$i\in I^j$}
        \State $(x^{\mathrm{unif}})_i \gets 1/|I^j|$
    \EndFor
\EndFor

\State Define $f(x)=\|Rx\|^2+q^Tx$
\State Define $\nabla f(x)=2R^T(Rx)+q$

\State Call SLSQP from $x^{\mathrm{unif}}$ on
$\min f(x)$ subject to $Ax=\mathbf{1}$ and $x\geq 0$

\State Store the returned point $x^{A2,\mathrm{cold}}$,
objective value, status, iterations, timing, and residuals

\State Call SLSQP from $x^{A1}$ on
$\min f(x)$ subject to $Ax=\mathbf{1}$ and $x\geq 0$

\State Store the returned point $x^{A2,\mathrm{warm}}$,
objective value, status, iterations, timing, and residuals

\State Compute
$t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
\gets
t_{A1}+t_{A2,\mathrm{warm}}$

\State \Return
$x^{A2,\mathrm{cold}}$,
$x^{A2,\mathrm{warm}}$,
their objective values,
statuses,
iterations,
timings,
residuals,
and $t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{Computational cost}

One objective evaluation requires $Rx$ and costs $O(\nnz(R)+n)$ in sparse arithmetic or $O(rn+n)$ for dense $R$. One gradient evaluation additionally computes $R^T(Rx)$ and has the same leading order. The block constraints cost $O(n)$ to evaluate. The total A2 cost also contains the dense linear-algebra work internal to SLSQP, which depends on the active set and cannot be summarized by a single problem-independent iteration bound. The numerical results report the number of SLSQP iterations,
objective-function and gradient evaluations, and wall-clock time.

\subsection{Parameters}

The main A1 configuration uses
\[

\qquad

\qquad
\delta_0=1,
\qquad
\eta=0.5,
\qquad
\alpha_{\min}=0.1,
\qquad
T_{\max}=5000.
\]
These values satisfy the theoretical conditions stated in
Section~2. Their finite-iteration effect is examined in the parameter
sensitivity experiment.

For A2, both the cold-start and warm-start runs use SciPy SLSQP with
the same settings:
\[
\texttt{ftol}=10^{-10},
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{A2}}=800.
\]
The cold-start run uses the uniform feasible point
\[
(x^0_{\mathrm{cold}})_i=\frac{1}{|I_k|},
\qquad i\in I_k,
\]
whereas the warm-start run uses
\[
x^0_{\mathrm{warm}}=x_{\mathrm{A1}}.
\]
All remaining solver options, objective and gradient functions,
constraints, and bounds are identical. Consequently, any difference
between the two A2 runs is attributable to the initial point and to
ordinary runtime variability.

The reported wall-clock times are obtained from one execution of each
solver configuration on each generated instance. Therefore, the timing
results should be interpreted as implementation-level measurements on
the current hardware rather than as precise performance estimates.
This limitation is particularly relevant for the smallest instances,
for which the A2 execution time is only a few milliseconds.

The reported A1 time includes both the target-value deflected dual
phase and the subsequent conditional-gradient polishing phase. The
A2-cold and A2-warm times contain only the corresponding SLSQP calls.
The end-to-end warm-start pipeline time is therefore computed as
\[
t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
=
t_{\mathrm{A1}}+t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}.
\]

\subsection{Instance generation}

The numerical instances are generated through a factor
$R\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}$, so that
\[
Q=R^TR
\]
is positive semidefinite by construction. Before applying the density
mask, the entries are sampled independently according to
\[
R_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),
\qquad
q_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]

When the prescribed density is smaller than one, a random binary mask
is applied to the entries of $R$. The resulting matrix is then scaled
by
\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{r\rho_R}},
\]
where $\rho_R$ denotes the target density. This scaling keeps the
quadratic term at a comparable numerical magnitude across the tested
families.

The four instance families used in the reported experiments are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{small dense}: $n=50$, $m=5$, block size $10$,
    $r=20$, density $1.00$;

    \item \textbf{medium dense}: $n=100$, $m=10$, block size $10$,
    $r=35$, density $1.00$;

    \item \textbf{medium sparse}: $n=150$, $m=15$, block size $10$,
    $r=45$, density $0.20$;

    \item \textbf{large sparse}: $n=160$, $m=20$, block size $8$,
    $r=50$, density $0.12$.
\end{itemize}

Two independent random seeds are used for every family. The generated
instances are stored in
\texttt{code/results/data/}, so that all reported comparisons can be
repeated on exactly the same numerical inputs.

The labels ``medium sparse'' and ``large sparse'' refer to the zero
pattern and target density of $R$. For portability, the current
implementation still stores and processes the masked matrices as dense
NumPy arrays. Therefore, the reported wall-clock times are based on
dense linear algebra. The
$O(\operatorname{nnz}(R))$ complexity discussed in
Sections~2.7 and~3.4 refers to an implementation using an actual sparse
matrix representation.

\subsection{Reported metrics}

For every test instance, the experiments report the following
quantities:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the best feasible objective value produced by A1 after
    conditional-gradient polishing;

    \item the record dual lower bound of A1 and the relative
    primal--dual certificate
    \[
    \operatorname{gap}_{\mathrm{A1}}
    =
    \frac{UB-LB}{\max\{1,|UB|\}};
    \]

    \item the objective values, iteration counts, solver statuses,
    elapsed times, feasibility residuals, and blockwise KKT residuals
    of A2-cold and A2-warm;

    \item the relative objective discrepancies
    \[
    \Delta_{\mathrm{cold}}
    =
    \frac{
    |f_{\mathrm{A1}}-f_{\mathrm{A2,cold}}|
    }{
    \max\{1,|f_{\mathrm{A2,cold}}|\}
    },
    \]
    and
    \[
    \Delta_{\mathrm{warm}}
    =
    \frac{
    |f_{\mathrm{A1}}-f_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}|
    }{
    \max\{1,|f_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}|\}
    };
    \]

    \item the warm-start speedup
    \begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{warm}}
    =
    \frac{
    t_{\mathrm{A2,cold}}
    }{
    t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}
    };
    \label{eq:warm-speedup}
    \end{equation}

    \item the end-to-end A1--A2 pipeline time
    \[
    t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
    =
    t_{\mathrm{A1}}+t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}};
    \]

    \item the pipeline-to-cold ratio
    \begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
    =
    \frac{
    t_{\mathrm{pipeline}}
    }{
    t_{\mathrm{A2,cold}}
    }.
    \label{eq:pipeline-ratio}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

A value $S_{\mathrm{warm}}>1$ means that warm-starting decreases the
internal A2 refinement time. A value
$R_{\mathrm{pipeline}}<1$ means that the complete A1--A2 pipeline is
faster than independently running A2 from its default initial point.
The quantities answer different questions and are therefore reported
separately.

\subsection{Projection diagnostics}

The projection onto $B_\rho$ is included to guarantee that the
multiplier sequence remains in a bounded set containing a dual
optimum. Consequently, the subgradients and deflected directions are
uniformly bounded, as required by the convergence theorem.

The recorded multiplier norms remain below the radius $\rho$ on the
tested instances. This confirms that every stored iterate satisfies
the imposed ball constraint. However, the current implementation does
not separately record the norm of every unprojected trial point or the
number of iterations at which the projection changes the trial point.
Therefore, no numerical claim is made here about how often the
projection is active.

Regardless of its observed numerical activity, the projection remains
essential to the theoretical analysis because it provides an explicit
bounded feasible set for the dual iteration.

All tested configurations produce objective values close to the
A2-cold reference solution. The absolute relative objective
differences remain of order $10^{-5}$ or smaller. The corresponding
KKT residuals are also small, indicating that the polished A1 points
are close to satisfying the first-order optimality conditions.

The results show that the algorithmic parameters mainly affect the
finite-iteration primal--dual certificate rather than the final
objective value. In particular, the tested value
\[
\nu_0=0.5
\]
produces the smallest certificate among the three tested initial
stepsizes. The smaller value $\nu_0=0.2$ gives slower progress within
the fixed iteration budget, while the larger value $\nu_0=1.0$ leads
to a somewhat larger final objective discrepancy and KKT residual.

The tested values of the stepsize exponent $p$ also produce similar
final objective values, but different primal--dual certificates.
This is consistent with the role of $p$ in controlling the rate at
which the stepsize
\[
\nu_k=\frac{\nu_0}{k^p}
\]
decreases. A smaller value of $p$ preserves larger steps for longer,
whereas a larger value decreases the steps more rapidly.

Increasing $\alpha_{\min}$ from $0.1$ to $0.3$ changes the minimum
weight assigned to the current subgradient in the deflected direction.
On this test instance, the change has only a limited effect on the
objective value, certificate, and KKT residual.

\subsection{Native PSD input check}

The main experiments generate the quadratic matrix through a factor
$R$, so that $Q=R^TR$ is positive semidefinite by construction.
Nevertheless, the implementation also accepts a symmetric positive
semidefinite matrix $Q$ directly. In this case,
\texttt{SimplexQP.from\_Q} reconstructs a factor $R$ using the
symmetric eigendecomposition described in~(5), retaining only the
eigenvalues larger than the prescribed numerical tolerance.

A dedicated PSD-input validation is implemented in
\texttt{scripts/make\_report\_assets.py}. It constructs an instance
from a factor $R$, reconstructs a second representation from the
corresponding matrix $Q=R^TR$, and compares
\[
x^TQx+q^Tx
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\|Rx\|_2^2+q^Tx
\]
on twenty randomly generated feasible points. The maximum absolute
objective discrepancy is written to
\texttt{code/results/psd\_factorization\_check.txt}. The observed
discrepancy is at the level of floating-point roundoff, confirming that
the native-$Q$ representation and the factorized representation define
the same quadratic objective up to numerical precision.

Separately, \texttt{scripts/run\_self\_check.py} verifies the same
factorization on the uniform feasible point before performing the A1
and A2 consistency checks.

\section{Discussion}

Dualizing the linking equality $y=Rx$ makes every A1 Lagrangian
relaxation exactly solvable. For a fixed multiplier, the
$y$-subproblem has a closed-form solution, while the $x$-subproblem
reduces to one minimum reduced-cost search on each simplex block.
Thus, A1 satisfies the no-solver requirement. Directly dualizing the
simplex equalities would instead leave a nonnegative quadratic
optimization problem inside the oracle.

The theoretical guarantee concerns the record dual lower-bound
sequence. Under the assumptions verified in Section~2, this sequence
converges asymptotically to the primal optimum. The result does not
imply monotonicity of the individual dual values or provide a
worst-case convergence rate for the implemented deflected
subgradient variant.

Every reported A1 upper bound is obtained from an explicitly feasible
point. Together with the record dual lower bound, this gives the valid
certificate
\[
\frac{UB-LB}{\max\{1,|UB|\}}.
\]
Because the exact Lagrangian solutions are simplex vertices, running
averages and conditional-gradient polishing are used to obtain better
primal points when the optimum is not a vertex. In the experiments,
most A1 runs reach the iteration limit before satisfying the requested
certificate, but they still return valid lower and upper bounds without
claiming convergence.

The A2 experiments separate two different questions. A2-cold starts
from the uniform feasible point and is the appropriate reference for
comparing A1 and A2 as independent solvers. A2-warm starts from the
polished A1 solution and measures the benefit of initialization. On the
tested instances, warm-starting reduces the SLSQP refinement time, but
the complete cost
\[
t_{\mathrm{A1}}+t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}
\]
is larger than the A2-cold time on every tested family. Hence, running
A1 solely to initialize A2 is not computationally advantageous on this
experiment grid.

The methods nevertheless provide different information. A2-cold has
the lowest measured wall-clock time, whereas A1 additionally returns a
valid dual lower bound and a primal--dual certificate. A2-warm remains
useful when an A1 solution has already been computed for certification
or analysis and only an additional primal refinement is desired.

These timing conclusions are specific to the current implementation,
hardware, solver settings, stopping rules, and test instances. Since
each configuration is timed once and some A2 runs last only a few
milliseconds, the reported ratios are implementation-level
observations rather than general performance or complexity claims.

\section{Conclusion}

A1 is a solver-free Lagrangian dual decomposition for convex quadratic
programmes over a product of simplices. Its oracle is solved exactly,
the target-value deflected subgradient update satisfies the assumptions
of the cited convergence theorem, and projection onto an explicitly
constructed ball guarantees bounded multipliers and directions. A1
returns a feasible primal point, a valid dual lower bound, and a
primal--dual optimality certificate.

A2 applies SciPy's SLSQP routine directly to the original constrained
problem. To evaluate the effect of initialization fairly, it is run
both from the default uniform feasible point (A2-cold) and from the
polished A1 solution (A2-warm). The two configurations reach
essentially the same objective values, showing that initialization
mainly affects computational effort.

On the tested instances, A2-cold has the lowest measured wall-clock
time. Warm-starting reduces the A2 refinement time, but the complete
pipeline
\[
t_{\mathrm{A1}}+t_{\mathrm{A2,warm}}
\]
is slower than A2-cold on every tested family. Therefore, A1 should not
be run solely to initialize A2. Its main advantage is instead the
certified primal--dual information that SLSQP does not directly
provide.

The final comparison therefore distinguishes three roles: A2-cold is
the faster standalone implementation on the tested instances, A1
provides certification without a general-purpose solver, and A2-warm
offers an optional refinement when an A1 solution is already
available. These conclusions are empirical and specific to the
reported implementation and experimental setup.
